Two things worth money, free:  The advisor will tell you if your machine already carries a Windows license in its firmware — most do, which means you don’t need to buy Windows 11.  And RR Turbo, our light, private fork of Windows, is free forever.  The parts are the only thing you’ll spend on.

Scope:  We work Windows machines only — Macs are out of our lane.  The checker is read-only and runs entirely on your computer; nothing is uploaded, nothing is installed.

The Reference

What moves the needle.

The advisor does the thinking for your exact machine.  But if you want the lay of the land first, here’s what actually makes an old PC feel new—roughly in the order of what you’ll feel most.  Tap any one.

Storage — the one you’ll feel first
If your machine still has a spinning hard drive, this is the single biggest change you’ll feel—boot, apps, and files go from “make a coffee” to nearly instant.  Most machines take an NVMe drive (a gumstick that clicks into an M.2 slot) or a 2.5-inch SATA SSD.  The advisor checks which slots you actually have, so you don’t overpay for a Gen5 drive a Gen3 board can’t use.~$55 for 1TB · biggest felt upgrade
Memory (RAM)
Enough memory keeps the machine from choking when a few tabs and an app are open at once.  8GB is the floor, 16GB the sweet spot for most people—and honestly, most machines already have enough or just need one more stick.  On a desktop it’s a two-minute job; on a laptop it depends on the model (some have an open slot, some are soldered).  The advisor tells you the real ceiling before you buy.  Our own machines ship with room to grow—four slots, half of them open—so most owners won’t touch it for years.~$18–40 · often not needed yet
Wi-Fi card
Old Wi-Fi is a quiet tax on everything you do online.  A Wi-Fi 6/6E card (the Intel AX210 is the go-to) drops into the same socket on most laptops and desktops and makes the whole machine feel quicker on the internet.  Two landmines the advisor watches for: a card soldered down so it can’t be swapped, and a BIOS that rejects a non-approved card.  It flags both before you order.~$20 · easy win
Battery (laptops)
A laptop that only lasts twenty minutes isn’t really portable anymore—and that’s almost always a worn cell, not a dead machine.  A fresh battery (match the part number printed on the old one) brings it back.  One of the cheapest upgrades, and the one you feel every day you unplug.~$30–50 · laptops only
Graphics card (desktops)
A graphics card is the one upgrade with prerequisites—which is why the advisor checks them before it ever recommends one.  Three things have to line up: a PCIe slot with physical room for the card’s length, a power supply with headroom and the right connector, and enough case airflow to keep it cool.  Most small office desktops can’t power one—and that’s fine; they make superb everyday machines.  A proper tower usually can.  The advisor tells you which camp you’re in instead of selling you a card your box can’t run.Varies · check PSU + case first
Processor (CPU)
On most machines the CPU is the last thing you’ll need—and often the one you never do.  A chip from the last several years is still plenty for everyday work; it’s usually the drive, the memory, or the software holding things back, not the processor.  Where there’s room to grow (a socketed desktop CPU), the advisor will say so—but it’ll steer you to the cheap wins first.  Swapping a CPU is the most involved upgrade and rarely the one that moves the needle.Last resort, not first move
