Vapor pricing + FUD marketing

You're not being sold a computer.
You're being sold fear.

Skip to the deal-check tool ↓

The advertised machine is often sold out, or hundreds more once it's in your cart.  The scare that steers you off cheaper, better-built value is a script.  Here's how both tricks work — and a tool you can paste into any AI to check a deal yourself, including ours.

Myth"Budget power supplies catch fire."›

Every machine we build runs an 80+ Bronze power supply at minimum.  "80+" is a third-party certification — a unit earns it only by being sent to an independent lab and passing testing under real load.  The brands the internet loves to fear-monger — AresGame, Apevia, Raidmax — submitted to that testing and passed.  We then checked each one ourselves: strong single +12V rails, 25–50% headroom over measured peak draw, active PFC, full protection circuits, 3–5 year warranties.

The part nobody mentions: the budget OEM box people get steered toward frequently ships a PSU that is not 80+ anything — unrated, untested, sent to no lab.  The script attacks a certified unit as "unsafe" while waving through an uncertified one.

How we know: we didn't take it on faith.  We bought all three brands expecting junk — and found good fans, good finish, good power delivery.  We even bolted a full-size ATX board into a small case on a 500W unit to test the "won't handle a real graphics card" claim.  It ran fine.  Dozens deployed, 2.5 years in the field, zero failures.  A real safety defect produces a failure rate.  Ours is zero.

Myth"You need Gold or Platinum for that card."›

80+ Bronze / Silver / Gold / Platinum is an efficiency ladder — it measures how little power a unit wastes as heat at the wall, not how safely it runs your card.  Climbing a tier saves a few pennies a month in electricity.  Nothing more.

A graphics card doesn't "need" Gold.  It needs three things, all of which a properly-sized Bronze unit delivers: correct sizing, a sufficient +12V rail, and enough headroom to ride out surges.  We scope every build to the real rated 12V output — the number the certification makes trustworthy — not the top-line sticker.  Efficiency is a fair preference.  Selling it as a requirement is just fear with a price tag.

Myth"That CPU is too old."›

Old for what?  The honest question is the workload.  For everyday computing and the games and sims most people run, a well-chosen last-gen chip has power to spare — and it's paired with a full 32GB of memory while the "new" $850 box ships 16GB.

And ours are standard ATX — genuinely upgradeable.  A newer card, more storage, more memory drop right in.  The proprietary OEM box can't say that.  "Old" is doing a lot of work in that sentence; usually it means "we'd rather you paid for new."

The honest partWhere we lose — and we'll tell you.›

We're not pretending we win every comparison.  If you want the absolute highest frame rates in the newest titles and you're willing to pay current-silicon prices for them, a new machine with a current GPU will beat ours on raw performance.  If raw benchmark numbers are the only thing you care about, buy that.

What we won't do is scare you off good value with a myth, or quote you a price that isn't real.  The tool below will tell you plainly when the other machine is the better buy for you.  That's the whole point.
